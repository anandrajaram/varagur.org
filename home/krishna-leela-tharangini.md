The Krishna Leela Tharangini is a Sanskrit musical opera on the life of Lord Krishna. Sri Narayana Theerthar composed it at Varagur in the early 18th century, drawing the narrative from the tenth skandha of the Bhagavata Purana. The composer divided the work into twelve sections he called *tarangams* — meaning "waves". Each one is a self-contained section of songs, Sanskrit verses, and prose that moves the story forward by one stage of Krishna's life.

## The twelve tarangams

The work moves through twelve waves, from Krishna's birth at Mathura to his marriage with Rukmini at Dwaraka. The episodes below follow the broad arc preserved in the printed editions; the exact section headings and song counts vary by *pathantara* (see the note further down). ## What is a tarangam?

A tarangam (Sanskrit: *taraṅga*, "wave") is the structural unit of the Tharangini. Each tarangam combines three kinds of material:

- **Gita** — songs set to specific ragas, intended for melodic recitation or stage performance.
- **Sloka** — Sanskrit verses, usually devotional praise or narrative summary.
- **Choornika** — passages of ornamented rhymed prose that link the songs together.

Across the twelve tarangams, the work contains 153 songs, 302 slokas, and 31 choornikas. The same word, *tarangam*, came to name a dance form in Kuchipudi where the performer interprets one of these songs while balancing on the rim of a brass plate.

## Performance traditions

The Tharangini is sung in three living traditions today:

- **Carnatic concert (kacheri)** — selected tarangams appear regularly in concert programmes. *Bala Gopala* (Mohanam), *Krishnam Kalaya Sakhi* (Mukhari), *Alokaye Rukmini* (Kambhoji), and *Sri Vasudeva Prabho* (Shankarabharanam) are standard repertoire pieces.
- **Kuchipudi** — the tarangam form in Kuchipudi takes its name from these songs. The dancer performs the song while balancing on the rim of a brass plate, often with a pot of water on the head, as a virtuoso climax to a recital.
- **Bhagavata Mela** — the Tamil dance-drama tradition of Melattur, Saliyamangalam, and Soolamangalam stages full episodes from the Tharangini each year, particularly around Narasimha Jayanti.

## The Varagur connection

Sri Narayana Theerthar composed the Tharangini during his years at Varagur. The tradition held at Sri Venkatesa Perumal Kovil is that when the saint sang the songs in the sannidhi, Lord Venkatesa danced to their tunes. Local tradition also holds that the saint heard the Lord's ankle bells (*salangai oli*) as he sang, and that Sri Anjaneya played the accompaniment. The Anjaneya Urchavam on the eleventh day of the [Uriyadi festival](/uriyadi/) each year is held in memory of this episode.

For the composer's life — including the white-pig journey from Andhra to Varagur and the Samadhi tradition — see the page on [Sri Narayana Theerthar](/home/sri-narayana-theerthar/).

## A note on pathantara

Printed and oral editions of the Tharangini differ in several places. The order of songs within a tarangam varies; some manuscripts include songs that others omit; ragas attributed to individual songs differ between the Tanjavur, Andhra, and Melattur traditions. The most thorough discussion of these *pathantara* variants is by V. Sriram of Madras Heritage and Carnatic Music, in his essay [The various pathantaras of the Sri Krishna Leela Tarangini](https://sriramv.com/2010/12/16/the-various-pathantaras-of-the-sri-krishna-leela-tarangini/).

The song pages on this site follow the 1913 printed edition of the work, available on the Internet Archive, with notes where significant variants exist.
